Sketch to Game Boy

A 15-second film of a Game Boy whose cartridge is a projector. Lights off, press A, and the game's blocks fall out of the wall and build a ring around it. Here is the full workflow, with the prompts, so you can make your own.

  • Claude Opus 5.5 (Claude Code)
  • Blender (free)
  • Python
  • HyperFrames (HTML to video)
  • ffmpeg

Step 1Break the idea into shots

Start from one sentence ("a Game Boy that projects games on my wall") and a reference film you like. Cut it into 6–8 shots of 1.5–3 seconds. Each shot gets one clear action. This is the shot list for this film:

Step 2One sketch per shot

The sketch is a director's note, not art. Draw the shape of the next moment, arrows for the motion, and one to three handwritten words for the action. If a sketch needs a paragraph to explain, split the shot.

  • Solid arrows mean motion, a dashed arrow means "this goes there".
  • Write real labels and numbers the shot will show ("CLEAR!", "press A").
  • In the split-screen version, the sketch draws itself with a pen while the 3D shot plays beside it.

Step 3Use real assets

Don't model the hero object from cubes; it always looks like a toy. Download a well-made model with a licence that allows your use, and credit the creator. Furniture, textures and studio lighting (HDRIs) are free under CC0 from Poly Haven.

Rule of thumb: CC0 = use anywhere. CC BY = credit the creator. CC BY-NC = credit, and no paid or client work.

Step 4Let Claude build the scene in Blender

Claude writes one Python script that builds the whole film: imports the models, sets lights and a camera per shot, keys every move, and renders with Cycles. You run it headless (blender -b -P scene.py) and look at test frames, then give notes.

Prompt · build the scene
Build a Blender 5 Python script that renders a 15 s, 30 fps, 9:16 film (720x1280 for tests).
Import [model.glb] as the hero, real scale. Two sets: a light studio cyc and a dark room with a round marble side table (Poly Haven).
One camera per shot, switched with timeline markers. Shots: [paste your shot list].
Keep two copies of the hero (one per set) and switch them with constant hide_render keys, so cuts have no motion-blur streaks.
Cycles GPU, 48 samples, OptiX denoise, motion blur on, AgX. Add --frames and --samples flags so I can render test frames.

Step 5Put a real game on the screen

The game is drawn frame by frame in Python at the Game Boy's own resolution, 160×144, in its four greens (#0F380F, #306230, #8BAC0F, #9BBC0F). The same image sequence feeds the console screen and the wall projection, so they always match. The blocks that fly out start exactly where they were drawn on the wall.

Step 6Check the physics frame by frame

The first versions looked fine at a glance and were wrong up close: the console floated 2 mm above the table, blocks sank up to 20 mm into it, and one flew through the console. Have Claude measure every frame before the final render.

Prompt · contact audit
Write an audit script I run after the scene script with --norender. For every frame:
- raycast the table top and report each resting object's lowest point vs the surface in mm,
- test every pair of blocks, and each block vs the console, for overlap with BVH trees,
- flag anything flying through the console.
Then fix the scene until every resting object is at 0.0 mm and there are zero overlaps.
0.0 mmEvery block, the heart and the console touch the table exactly. 0 overlapsBlocks keep a gap from each other and the console, in flight and at rest. Flat bottomsEach block lands on its widest side, so it could really stand there.

Step 7Overlays and the sketch card

The prompt box, the "Generating…" pill and the self-drawing sketch card are HTML, animated with a function of time and rendered to MP4 with HyperFrames. Text stays sharp, and the Blender film plays underneath as a video.

Step 8Sound that belongs to the world

  • Every effect is 8-bit: arcade pops, retro clicks, block hits, a coin sound for the heart.
  • The music is an original chiptune made from square waves and noise in code, with the beat hitting when the console lands and silence when the lights go off.
  • One sound per visual event, placed a frame before it, mixed to about −14 LUFS.
